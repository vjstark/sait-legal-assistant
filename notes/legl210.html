Unit 1 The three structures

1 · Why business structure matters

How a business is structured determines four things:

  • Who is liable for the business's torts, contracts, and debts;
  • Who collects a share of the profits or assets;
  • Who manages the business; and
  • Who is accountable for its management and performance.

There are three general ways to structure a business:

StructureSeparate legal entity?Owner liability
Sole proprietorshipNoUnlimited personal liability
PartnershipNoUnlimited, joint & several
CorporationYesLimited (to the value of shares)
The deciding factor

Liability is the biggest consideration. Because a corporation is a separate legal entity, its owners aren't personally on the hook for its debts — which is why incorporating is usually the most advisable way to structure a business.

CheckWhich of the three structures is a separate legal entity, and why does that matter?
Only the corporation.

Because it's a separate legal entity, the corporation — not its shareholders — is liable for its own debts and obligations. Sole proprietors and partners are their businesses, so they carry unlimited personal liability.

Unit 1 The three structures

2 · Sole proprietorship

A sole proprietorship is one person carrying on business in their own name. The owner is indistinguishable from the business — it is not a separate legal entity, and there is no line between the owner's assets and the business's assets. Contract with a sole proprietorship and you contract with the owner personally.

FeatureSole proprietorship
LiabilityUnlimited personal liability — personal assets can be seized to pay business debts
ProfitsAll after-tax profits belong to the owner
DecisionsTotal control (may delegate to employees)
LifespanEnds when the owner dies or stops the business
TransferCan't be sold or inherited (only the assets can be transferred)
NameOwner's name by default; may use a trade name
TaxesTaxed as personal income — usually a higher rate than a corporation
Trade-off

Simple, cheap, private, and fully in your control — but the unlimited liability means a business failure can mean personal bankruptcy, and it's hard to raise capital or attract talented staff (no ownership stake to offer).

CheckA sole proprietor's business is sued and loses. Can the plaintiff go after the owner's personal house?
Yes.

With unlimited personal liability and no separate legal entity, the owner's personal assets — including their home — can be seized to satisfy a business debt or damages award.

3 · Partnership

A partnership is two or more persons carrying on business together with the goal to make a profit. All three elements — (1) two or more persons, (2) carrying on business together, (3) to make a profit — and a partnership is automatically deemed to exist, whether or not the parties intended it.

The unintended-partnership trap

Because it forms automatically, you can create a partnership without meaning to. Disputes usually erupt over profits (each claims a partnership existed to get a share) or debts (each denies it to avoid liability). Choose partners carefully.

The Partnership Act sets the default rules; partners can override many of them with a Partnership Agreement.

FeatureDefault rule (Partnership Act)
LiabilityUnlimited, and joint & several — a plaintiff can sue and collect from one, some, or all partners (you may pay for a partner's wrongdoing, then recover their share)
ProfitsShared equally (an agreement can make sharing unequal)
DecisionsMost decisions by majority vote
DissolutionEnds on expiry, a partner's notice, or a partner's death, insanity, or bankruptcy. On dissolution, assets pay debts first; if short, partners contribute in proportion to their profit share
CheckYour partner runs up a business debt without you. Can a creditor collect the whole amount from you?
Yes — joint & several liability.

A creditor can pursue any partner for the full amount. You'd then be entitled to recover the other partners' proportional shares from them — but you're still on the hook to the creditor first. That's why partner choice matters so much.

4 · Limited partnerships & LLPs

Two special partnership subtypes limit liability in different ways:

Limited Partnership (LP)Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)
Who's protectedLimited partners have limited liability but cannot take part in management. General partners manage and keep unlimited liability.Every partner is shielded from a fellow partner's negligence.
Extent of protectionLimited partners risk only their investment.A partner is liable only for their own negligence (and employees directly under them). For other debts, partners are still jointly & severally liable.
Formed byFiling specific paperwork.Filing; must carry liability insurance for negligence.
Typical usePassive investors + an active manager.Professionals — lawyers, accountants — whose biggest risk is a negligence suit.
Why an LLP helps professionals

In an LLP, one partner's negligence lawsuit doesn't threaten the whole firm — each partner answers only for their own negligence. But breach of contract and other debts still expose partners fully.

CheckIn an LLP law firm, Partner A is sued for negligence. Is Partner B's personal wealth at risk for A's negligence?
No — not for A's negligence.

An LLP shields partners from each other's negligence, so B isn't personally liable for A's negligence. (B would still be exposed for other kinds of firm debt, which remain joint & several.)

Unit 1 Corporations

5 · The corporation — separate legal entity & limited liability

The corporation is the most important modern business structure and works completely differently from the others. Its owners are shareholders. ("Corporation" and "company" mean the same thing.)

A separate legal entity ("corporate personhood")

A corporation is treated as its own "person" for many purposes — it can sue and be sued, enter contracts, commit torts, own property, and pay taxes in its own name. It is liable for its own debts and obligations.

Limited liability: because the corporation is separate from its owners, shareholders are not personally liable for its debts. The most a shareholder can lose is the value of their shares.

Kim buys 10 shares of Telus for $200. Telus is later sued for $200 million and owes $500 million in debt.
Kim's liability is capped at his $200 — his shares could drop to $0, but his personal assets are safe.
The landmark case: Salomon v. Salomon

Mr. Salomon incorporated his shoe business as Salomon Ltd. When the company became insolvent, its creditors tried to make Mr. Salomon pay its debts. The House of Lords held he was not liable: Mr. Salomon and Salomon Ltd were two different legal entities, so only the company's own assets answered for its debts. This separate legal personality is arguably the single most important feature of a corporation.

CheckA corporation defaults on a $1M loan. Its sole shareholder has a $2M house. Can the lender seize the house?
No.

Only assets held in the corporation's own name can be seized. The shareholder's personal assets are protected by limited liability — the principle confirmed in Salomon v. Salomon.

6 · Governance & dividends

Shareholders own the corporation but don't manage it directly. Three roles run it:

RoleChosen byJob & duty
Shareholders—Own shares; elect directors. No inherent right to dividends.
DirectorsElected by shareholdersHighest level of management; set strategy; declare dividends. Owe a fiduciary duty to the corporation (its best interest, not shareholders' short-term gains).
Officers (e.g. CEO)Appointed by directorsRun day-to-day operations; are employees; also owe a fiduciary duty to the corporation.

One person can be a shareholder, director, and officer. Regular employees do not owe a fiduciary duty — only directors and officers do.

Dividends: a right that switches on

Shareholders make money two ways: the share price rising, or dividends. Shareholders have no right to a dividend until the directors declare one (in writing). Once declared, the right activates and they can collect; once paid, it switches off until directors declare again. Some corporations never pay dividends.

CheckYou own shares but got no dividend this year. Can you force the company to pay one? Who do directors' duties run to?
No — and directors owe their duty to the corporation.

Dividends are only owed once declared by the directors; there's no inherent right to them. Directors owe a fiduciary duty to the corporation itself, not to shareholders' short-term interests.

7 · Corporate features & trade-offs

FeatureWhat it means
Perpetual existenceBeing a separate legal entity with transferable shares, a corporation exists until it is dissolved — it doesn't die with an owner.
Share transferPublic corporations: shares are freely bought, sold, and gifted. Private corporations: a shareholder agreement restricts transfers.
Equity financingThe corporation can issue more shares to raise capital — no repayment obligation, unlike a loan.
NamingRequires checking name availability across jurisdictions and against trademarks.
Double taxationDividends are taxed twice: at the (low) corporate rate, then again at the shareholder's (higher) personal rate. Low corporate rates encourage reinvestment over payout.
The bottom line

Corporations carry more regulation and complexity, but usually offer a net benefit. The headline advantage is limited liability, plus flexible ownership, perpetual existence, and easier access to capital.

CheckWhat is "double taxation," and what's the corporation's single biggest advantage?
Profits taxed at the corporate rate, then dividends taxed again personally — and the biggest advantage is limited liability.

The corporation pays tax on its profits; when it distributes dividends, shareholders pay personal tax on them too. Despite that and the added complexity, limited liability (shareholders aren't personally on the hook) is the corporation's defining benefit.
