Foundations Law & lawmaking

1 · Rules vs. laws

We are socialized through a sea of rules — at home, school, work, and in the community — that shape and govern our behaviour morally, ethically, socially, and financially. Parents, teachers, bosses, and society make them.

Laws are also rules of conduct meant to guide behaviour in society — but they are created by government (federal or provincial, and sometimes the courts) and are enforceable by government, government agencies such as the police, and the courts, with legally sanctioned penalties or remedies for non-compliance.

The one line to remember

"All laws are rules, but not all rules are laws." Breaking a plain rule may have consequences, but they are not legally enforceable. Breaking a law carries legally enforceable penalties (sanctions).

RulesLaws
Who makes themParents, teachers, employers, communityGovernment (federal/provincial); sometimes the courts
Who enforcesFamily, school, workplace, social pressureGovernment, police, the courts
ConsequencesReal, but not legally enforceableLegally enforceable penalties / remedies
From the lecture

"Morality cannot be legislated, but behaviour can be regulated… Judicial decrees may not change the heart, but they can restrain the heartless." — Martin Luther King, Jr. In short: you can legislate a change of behaviour, but you cannot legislate a change of heart.

CheckWhat is the single biggest difference between a rule and a law?
Legal enforceability.

Both guide conduct, but a law is created by government and carries legally enforceable penalties enforced by the courts, police, and government. A rule's consequences are not legally enforceable.

2 · How a rule becomes law (the "journey")

The journey from a rule of society to an enforceable law begins when government has the political will to act on a perceived need — the classic example is distracted driving: as cell-phone use pushed up accidents, claims, and ER visits, a rule of the road became a law.

It then travels through all three branches of government:

StepBranchWhat happens
1ExecutiveThe relevant department drafts the Bill and presents it to the legislature.
2LegislativeThe Bill is voted on in three readings (First, Second, Third). A majority government will usually pass it.
3—It becomes an Act (Statute), but is not yet law: it must be proclaimed in force by the Premier/PM and receive royal assent — the King's representative signs it (Lieutenant Governor provincially / Governor General federally).
4JudicialThe courts enforce and interpret the Act — "breathing life" into its formal words by applying it to the facts and evidence of each case.
Draft vs. vote vs. enforce

The Executive drafts, the Legislative votes it into law, and the Judicial branch enforces and interprets it. A Bill only becomes enforceable law after three readings, royal assent, and proclamation in force.

CheckA Bill has passed three readings in the Legislature. Is it law yet?
Not quite.

After passing, it is an Act/Statute, but it is not enforceable law until it receives royal assent (signed by the King's representative) and is proclaimed in force by the Premier or Prime Minister.

3 · The three branches of government

Both the federal and provincial governments have the same three branches:

BranchWhoRole
LegislativeElected MPs (federal) / MLAs (provincial)Debate and vote bills into law.
ExecutivePM (federal) / Premier (provincial) + their CabinetRun government departments (Ministers of Health, Education, Transportation…) and initiate/draft legislation.
JudicialJustices — appointed, not electedInterpret and apply the law; create case law (judge-made / common law).
Judicial independence

Justices are not elected and are not beholden to the other two branches for their decisions. Judicial independence keeps politicians (and the public) from influencing or bribing a justice — it is a cornerstone of a free and democratic government.

CheckWhich branch drafts a bill, which votes it into law, and which enforces it?
Executive → Legislative → Judicial.

The Executive drafts, the Legislative votes, and the Judicial branch enforces and interprets — the last one independently of the other two.

4 · Two levels of government & the division of powers

A country with two levels of government is a federal system. The Constitution divides exclusive law-making powers between them:

Federal — s.91Provincial — s.92
Criminal law, marriage & divorce, currency, interest rates, the postal system, the military, international affairs, bankruptcy, penitentiariesProperty & civil rights, provincial roads & highways, incorporation of provincial companies, provincial taxation, municipalities (which pass by-laws), solemnization of marriage (the licence & ceremony)

How each legislature is built

  • Federal Parliament is bicameral (two houses): the Senate (appointed) and the House of Commons (elected — ~338 MPs for 338 ridings across Canada).
  • Alberta's legislature is unicameral (one house): the Legislative Assembly (~87 elected MLAs for 87 districts). Bills still require three readings, just like federally.
CheckIs criminal law a federal or provincial power? What about municipalities?
Criminal law = federal (s.91); municipalities = provincial (s.92).

Criminal law is one of the enumerated federal powers under s.91. Municipal governments are created by the provinces under s.92 and pass by-laws. (Note: highway-safety matters like distracted driving are provincial.)

5 · The Constitution & the Charter

The Constitution is the blueprint for how Canada is governed, and it is the supreme law of Canada. It was repatriated in 1982; the old BNA Act 1867 became the Constitution Act, 1867, and the Charter of Rights and Freedoms was created in 1982 and embedded in the Constitution Act, 1982.

The three supreme sources of law

Together, the Constitution Act 1867, the Constitution Act 1982, and the Charter of Rights and Freedoms are supreme over all federal and provincial laws and all judge-made case law — including the Supreme Court of Canada.

What the Constitution does

  • Outlines the structure of Parliament (bicameral), how elections work, and the role of the monarchy.
  • Divides law-making powers — federal (s.91) and provincial (s.92).
  • Creates the three branches of government and upholds judicial independence.
  • Sets up the two levels of government (a federal system).
  • Establishes parliamentary supremacy: government-made statutes overrule common law (even the SCC) — subject to compliance with the Charter.
What the Charter guarantees

The Charter is a judicially enforceable guarantee that government acts consistently with a free and democratic society. Examples: fundamental freedoms (conscience & religion, thought/belief/expression, peaceful assembly, association), the right to a fair and speedy trial, equality rights, and the right to vote.

CheckName the three supreme sources of law in Canada — and is the Charter part of the Constitution?
Constitution Act 1867 · Constitution Act 1982 · Charter of Rights and Freedoms — yes, the Charter is part of the Constitution.

The Charter was created in 1982 and embedded in the Constitution Act, 1982, so it shares the Constitution's supreme status. All government and judge-made law must comply with it.

6 · Classifying law

Two legal systems

  • Common law — no written code; follows prior judge-made case law on similar facts through the doctrine of precedent. Used across Canada.
  • Civil law — a written, codified body of legal principles applied by the courts. Used in Quebec (provincial matters only — a "bijural" system) and in Louisiana.

How we group laws

SplitCategories
ReachInternational (Public — governments/countries, e.g. the International Court of Justice in The Hague; Private — companies/citizens across borders) vs Domestic (the laws of Canada)
Substance vs. processSubstantive law = your R.O.D. — Rights, Obligations & Duties (what you must do, can't do, and can expect). Procedural law = the rules that enforce substantive law, ensuring accountability and due process.
Who's involvedPrivate law (between citizens/companies — contract, property, employment, corporate) vs Public law (government is a party — criminal, constitutional, tax, immigration, intellectual property)
Why procedure matters

If police fail to follow procedural rules — e.g. reading an accused their Charter rights (right to counsel, right to silence) — a court can refuse to admit even a voluntary confession. Procedure protects due process, not just the outcome.

CheckIs contract law public or private? Is a rule about how to serve court documents substantive or procedural?
Contract law = private; a rule about serving documents = procedural.

Contract law governs disputes between private parties (no government as a party). A rule about how a right is enforced or carried out is procedural; the right itself (R.O.D.) is substantive.

7 · Precedent & the Alberta courts

Precedent

Higher courts bind lower ones. A Supreme Court of Canada decision "on point" binds every court in the country — that's precedent. But government-made law trumps all case law (even the SCC), subject to the Charter.

Alberta's court hierarchy, from the highest trial court up to the top appeal court:

CourtWhat it doesJustices
Alberta Court of Justice (ACJ)
lower trial court		Five divisions: Civil / Small Claims (up to $100,000), Criminal (first appearances + lower matters), Traffic (Justices of the Peace), Youth (ages 12 to under 18), Family (support, parenting, guardianship)Appointed by the province
Alberta King's Bench (KB)
highest trial court		Serious criminal cases, civil cases over $100,000, adoptions, matrimonial property, estates & wills. The only court with jury trials.Appointed by the federal government
Alberta Court of Appeal
highest AB appeal court		Not a trial court — no new evidence. Reviews questions of law and fact from King's Bench; can vary, substitute, reverse, or uphold. Usually a panel of three.Appointed by the federal government
Supreme Court of Canada (SCC)
highest in Canada		Highest appeals court in the country; needs leave (permission) to appeal; hears matters of national importance, new law, and constitutional questions. Binds all Canadian courts.Nine, appointed by the federal government
CheckWhich Alberta court hears jury trials, and how many justices sit on the Supreme Court of Canada?
Alberta King's Bench holds jury trials; the SCC has nine justices.

King's Bench is the only Alberta court with jury trials (it's the highest trial court). The Supreme Court of Canada has nine federally-appointed justices and requires leave to appeal.
