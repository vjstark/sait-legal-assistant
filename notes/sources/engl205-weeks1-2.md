## 1. What is grammar? Prescriptive vs. descriptive

**Grammar** is the set of rules that tells us how words relate to one another to make meaning. It is why *Sally bit my dog* tells us Sally did the biting: English word order puts the **subject** (the doer) before the **verb**. Scramble the words — *today have go work to I to is* — and the meaning collapses.

"Rule" means two different things:

- **Descriptive rules** describe how English *actually works* in real usage — the rules that turn your ~42,000 words into meaning. They are studied by linguists, vary by *community of speakers*, and change over time. Example: *He ran real quick* is perfectly grammatical in the communities that speak that way.
- **Prescriptive rules** are rules of "proper" usage — grammatical etiquette promulgated by teachers, editors, and style guides. Examples: use *whom* as an object; say *May I…* rather than *Can I…*; avoid *her and I*. They are socially important (like table manners), but not always logically justified — the reading calls weak justifications "soup logic" (like the "proper" way to eat soup from the back of the bowl).

**Who makes the rules?** Mostly all of us — speakers, friends, family, influencers, plus the prescriptivists. This course mostly "looks under the hood" (descriptive) so you can *explain why* commas, sentence boundaries, and errors work the way they do — while still writing to the standard (prescriptive) conventions expected in professional and legal work.

**Self-check:** Is "Adding -s makes most nouns plural" prescriptive or descriptive? → **Descriptive** (it states a fact about how the language behaves).

## 2. Why proofreading is hard (and how to do it)

Proofreading your own writing is hard because:

- **Your brain auto-corrects.** You can easily read *the olny iprmoatnt tihng is taht the frist and lsat ltteer are in the rghit pcale* — so your eyes glide over real errors too.
- **English is inconsistent.** It borrows spelling and rules from Latin, French, Greek, and others.
- **Spellcheck misses things**, especially real words used in the wrong place.

**Why it matters:** in legal/official documents a tiny mark changes meaning. In the **Oakhurst Dairy** case, a missing Oxford comma in an overtime law cost the company millions.

**Proofreading techniques:**
- Read slowly and **aloud**, or use text-to-speech — forces you to process every word.
- Read it **backward, word by word** — breaks your brain's autocorrect.
- Change the font, size, or layout; **print** it — makes familiar text look new.
- Use a ruler / highlight one line at a time — stops your eye jumping ahead.
- Circle every punctuation mark — isolates commas, periods, quotes.
- Step away, then review later — distance restores fresh eyes.

**Limits of AI/spellcheckers:** they miss real errors, give inconsistent results, and can "hallucinate" errors that aren't there; they may quietly apply US conventions. Don't rely on them for complex or legal documents — use them as a second pass only.

**Self-check:** Which technique best defeats auto-correcting as you read? → **Reading backward, word by word.**

## 3. Common error types to hunt for

**Confusing words:**
- **Homophones** — sound the same; different meaning/spelling (*your / you're*; *their / there / they're*).
- **Homographs** — spelled the same; different meaning/pronunciation (*read* present vs *read* past).
- **Homonyms** — same spelling *and* sound; different meaning (*ring* jewelry vs *ring* call).

**Spelling & letters:**
- **Reversed/dropped letters** (*form* vs *from*; *trial* vs *trail*).
- **EI vs. IE** — "i before e, except after c": *receive, believe* (many exceptions — verify).
- **Hyphenated/compound words** — a *compound adjective* before a noun takes a hyphen: a *human-like voice*. Without it the meaning blurs.
- **Capitalization** — proper nouns, titles, sentence starts; watch style rules for capitalization after a colon.

**Canadian vs. American vs. British spelling.** Canadian English is a mix: it usually keeps British `-our`/`-re` but takes American `-ize` and some American words.

| American | British | Canadian |
|---|---|---|
| color | colour | colour |
| apologize | apologise | apologize |
| tire | tyre | tire |
| gray | grey | grey |

**Punctuation & style consistency:** style guides disagree — pick one and stay consistent. American style puts commas/periods *inside* quotation marks ("Her,"); British style often *outside* ("Her",). Guides also differ on the Oxford comma and italics vs. quotation marks for titles.

**Self-check:** Canadian spelling of *color*? → **colour.** Is it *apologize* or *apologise*? → **apologize** (Canadian keeps British `-our` but uses American `-ize`).

## 4. Parts of speech & how to categorize a word

Every word is filed into a **word class** (part of speech). This course uses an eight-class scheme (plus interjections). Schemes differ between grammars — sorting 170,000 words is like sorting paint chips into "red, orange, yellow…"; boundaries are somewhat arbitrary, so some words sit on the line.

The working scheme, with examples:

| Class | Example | Class | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Noun | student | Determiner (determinative) | the |
| Pronoun | they | Preposition | under |
| Verb | learn | Subordinator | because |
| Adjective | precise | Coordinator | and |
| Adverb | carefully | Interjection | wow |

**The four clues** (combine them; always check in context):
- **Meaning** — what does it express? Useful, but *never enough on its own.*
- **Form** — what endings can it take? (plural `-s`, possessive `-'s`, `-ly`, `-ous`, `-er/-est`).
- **Position** — where can it sit? (after a determiner, before a noun, etc.).
- **Context** — the whole sentence settles it.

**Your "grammar brain" already knows this.** In *The froominous meeps garkel voominously*, you can label every made-up word by position and form, not meaning: *froominous* = adjective, *meeps* = noun, *garkel* = verb, *voominously* = adverb.

**Self-check:** In that sentence, *garkel* is a **verb** — it sits after the subject noun phrase and before an `-ly` adverb, the slot only a verb fills.

## 5. Category vs. function

A giant screwdriver used to pry paint-can lids is *categorically* a screwdriver but *functionally* a prybar. Words are the same: a word's **category** (its word class) can differ from its **function** (the job it does in a sentence).

- *There must be a **dog** trainer…* — *dog* is categorically a noun but here **functions as an adjective** (an *adjectival*), describing *trainer*.

Function names:

| Category (word class) | Function name |
|---|---|
| Noun | Nominal |
| Adjective | Adjectival |
| Adverb | Adverbial |
| Determinative | Determiner |
| Verb, Pronoun, Coordinator, Subordinator | — (no separate function name; only they can do their job) |

Function names shine with **phrases**: in *The sasquatch **in the shed** is asking for a snack*, the phrase *in the shed* is an **adjectival** (it describes the sasquatch) even though it contains no adjective. So the more useful question is **"what is this word or phrase *doing*?"** rather than "what class is it?"

**Words drift** between categories: *adult* now works as a verb (*I have to adult today*); *access* became a verb in the computer age.

**Self-check:** Which has no separate function name: noun, adjective, or verb? → **Verb** (only a verb can do a verb's job; same for pronouns/coordinators/subordinators).

## 6. POSTs — Parts-of-Speech Tests

A **POST** is a sentence pattern with one controlled blank. Your grammar brain only allows certain words into the slot, so the pattern *tests* how a word can function — before you define anything. Fill *She ___ friendly* and you'll only reach for a **linking verb** (*is, seems, feels*), never *jumps*.

| To find a… | POST (fill the blank) | Words that fit |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | My ___ is/are important. | studies, health, skiing |
| Linking verb | The gorillas ___ curious. | is, are, seems, feels |
| Intransitive verb | She ___ expertly. | sings, swims, performs |
| Transitive verb | She ___ groceries frequently. | buys, orders, carries |
| Determiner (mostly) | ___ pretty flowers grew on the hill. | the, some, many, ten |
| Preposition (mostly) | The squirrel ___ the tree is annoyed. | in, on, under, behind |

**How to read a POST result:** it proves a *possible function in context*, not a permanent identity. One word can pass several (*turned* fits linking, intransitive, and transitive; *owe* only fits transitive). There are **no reliable POSTs** for pronouns, coordinators, or subordinators. The noun POST is just the linking-verb pattern with the *noun* left blank.

**Self-check:** In *"My ___ is/are important,"* which fails: studies, quickly, skiing, health? → **quickly** (an adverb). Note *skiing* fits but is a nominal.

## 7. Heads & dependents

Inside a phrase, one word is the boss:
- The **head** is the central word that determines what kind of phrase it is.
- A **dependent** is a word or group that adds to, completes, or limits the head. Remove the head and the phrase loses its anchor.

Example: *the · remarkably patient · **student** · near the window* — **head:** *student*; **dependents:** *the*, *remarkably patient*, *near the window*. Note *remarkably* depends on *patient*, not on *student*. Dependents can have their own dependents, and a dependent can be a whole phrase.

**The payoff — a comma rule:** **never separate a head from its dependent with a comma.** That explains the two wrong commas in *"Chinook Lodge will be hosting the first, in what we hope will be a series, of workshops…"* — they cut the head *first* off from its dependent *in what we hope will be a series of workshops*.

**Self-check:** Head of *"the remarkably patient student near the window"*? → **student.** *remarkably* depends on → **patient.**

## 8. Nouns & nominals

"Person, place, or thing" is too narrow. A **noun** can express:
- People & places (*Janelle, Calgary, SAIT*)
- Physical entities (*hamster, rainbow, crash*)
- Activities (*bowling, golf, badminton*)
- Concepts & qualities (*science, rigour, softness*)
- Mental processes (*dreams, worries, love, boredom*)

**Form & position clues:** most nouns have singular/plural and can take a possessive `-'s`; they often follow a determiner and head a noun phrase. You know *that merp likes the look of you* makes *merp* a noun purely from position — with no idea what a merp is.

A **nominal** is a word or phrase that isn't a word-class noun but *functions* in a noun slot. They pass the noun POST and can be replaced by *it*:
- *My **laptop** is important.* (noun)
- ***Skiing** is important.* (gerund)
- ***To learn** is important.* (to-infinitive)
- ***That you remembered my birthday** is important.* (whole clause)

A **gerund** is the `-ing` form doing a noun's job (*skiing, singing*).

**Self-check:** In *"Skiing is important,"* *skiing* is a **nominal (gerund)** — a verb form filling the noun/subject slot (passes the noun POST; replaceable by *it*).

## 9. Verb forms & the jobs they do

Every verb has a set of forms (its *paradigm*) — a closet of uniforms for different jobs. *show* has five:

| Form | Name | Main jobs |
|---|---|---|
| show | plain (base) | present tense; agreement (I/you/we/they); after a modal (*can show*); to-infinitive (*to show*) |
| shows | -s / third-person singular | present tense **+** agreement with he/she/it & singular nouns |
| showed | preterite (-ed) | simple past tense |
| shown | past participle (-en) | perfect verb strings (*have shown*); adjectival (*the paintings shown…*) |
| showing | present participle / gerund (-ing) | progressive verb strings (*is showing*); gerund/nominal; participle/modifier |

**Three things verb forms handle:**
- **Tense.** English changes *form* for only two tenses — **simple present** and **simple past**. Everything else uses a **verb string** (more than one verb): *will show, have shown, is showing, will have been waiting* (four verbs).
- **Agreement.** Only the `-s` form marks it, agreeing with *he/she/it* and singular nouns. There is no "plural verb," and the verb `-s` is *not* the noun plural `-s`. The verb *be* is the exception — 8 forms, 5 that show agreement: *am, is, are, was, were*.
- **"Non-verb" jobs.** The `-ing` form can be a gerund (nominal) or participle (adjectival/adverbial); the `-en` form can be adjectival; plain + *to* makes a to-infinitive acting as nominal/adjectival/adverbial.

**Self-check:** Preterite of *show*? → **showed.** Which form ends the perfect tenses (*have ___*)? → the past participle, **shown.** In *"the paintings shown in the gallery,"* *shown* is instead an **adjectival**.
