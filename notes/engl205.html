Week 1 Foundations

1 · What is grammar? Prescriptive vs. descriptive

Grammar is the set of rules that tells us how words relate to one another to make meaning. It is why Sally bit my dog tells us Sally did the biting — English word order puts the subject (the doer) before the verb. Scramble it — today have go work to I to is — and meaning collapses.

But "rule" means two very different things, and the course cares about the difference.

Descriptive rulesPrescriptive rules
How English actually works in real usage — the rules that turn your ~42,000 words into meaning. Rules of "proper" usage — grammatical etiquette from teachers, editors, and style guides.
Studied by linguists. Vary by community of speakers and change over time. e.g. use whom as an object; May I… not Can I…; avoid her and I.
He ran real quick is perfectly grammatical in the communities that speak that way. Socially important, like table manners — but not always logically justified ("soup logic").
Key idea

Who "makes the rules"? Mostly we all do — speakers, friends, family, influencers, and the prescriptivists. This course mostly "looks under the hood" (descriptive) so you can explain why commas, sentence boundaries, and errors work the way they do — while still writing to the standard (prescriptive) conventions expected in professional and legal work.

CheckIs this a prescriptive or descriptive rule: "Adding -s makes most nouns plural."
Descriptive.

It describes how English actually behaves — a fact about the language, not an etiquette rule. Compare a prescriptive rule like "use whom rather than who for an object," which tells you what is considered "proper."

2 · Why proofreading is hard (and how to do it)

Proofreading your own writing is hard for real reasons:

  • Your brain auto-corrects. You can read the olny iprmoatnt tihng is taht the frist and lsat ltteer are in the rghit pcale with no trouble — so your eyes glide over real errors too.
  • English is inconsistent. It borrows spelling and rules from Latin, French, Greek and more, so patterns don't always hold.
  • Spellcheck misses things. Microsoft Word doesn't catch every error, especially the ones that are real words in the wrong place.
Why it matters

In legal and official documents, a tiny mark changes meaning. In the Oakhurst Dairy case, a missing comma (the Oxford comma) in an overtime law cost the company millions. Punctuation is not decoration.

Proofreading techniques

TechniqueWhy it works
Read slowly and aloud, or use text-to-speechForces you to process every word instead of skimming.
Read it backward, word by wordBreaks your brain's autocorrect by removing the meaning/flow.
Change the font, size, or layout; print itMakes familiar text look new so errors resurface.
Use a ruler / highlight one line at a timeStops your eye from jumping ahead.
Circle every punctuation markIsolates commas, periods, and quotes for checking.
Step away, then review laterDistance restores your ability to see the page freshly.
On AI & spellcheckers

AI tools can help, but they miss real errors, give inconsistent results, and even "hallucinate" errors that aren't there — and they may quietly apply US conventions. Don't rely on them for complex or legal documents. Use them as a second pass, never the only pass.

CheckWhich single technique is best aimed at defeating your brain's tendency to auto-correct as you read?
Reading the text backward, word by word.

Removing the normal flow of meaning stops your brain from "filling in" what it expects to see, so misspellings and wrong words stand out. Reading aloud / text-to-speech helps for the same reason.

3 · Common error types to hunt for

Confusing words

TypeDefinitionExample
HomophonesSound the same; different meaning and/or spellingyour / you're · their / there / they're
HomographsSpelled the same; different meaning and/or pronunciationread (present) / read (past)
HomonymsSame spelling and sound; different meaningring (jewelry) / ring (call)

Spelling & letters

  • Reversed / dropped letters — e.g. form vs from, trial vs trail.
  • EI vs. IE — "i before e, except after c": receive, believe (with plenty of exceptions, so verify).
  • Hyphenated / compound words — a compound adjective before a noun takes a hyphen: a human-like voice (the hyphen ties human + like together to modify voice). Without it the meaning blurs.
  • Capitalization — proper nouns, titles, and sentence starts; watch style-guide rules for capitalization after a colon.

Canadian vs. American vs. British spelling

Canadian English is a mix: it usually keeps British -our and -re, but takes American -ize and some American words.

AmericanBritishCanadian
colorcolourcolour
apologizeapologiseapologize
tiretyretire
graygreygrey
Punctuation & style consistency

Style guides disagree, so pick one and stay consistent. For example, American style puts commas/periods inside quotation marks ("Her,"); British style often puts them outside ("Her",). Guides also differ on the Oxford comma and on italics vs. quotation marks for titles.

CheckWhich is the correct Canadian spelling: color, colour, or coler? And is it apologize or apologise?
colour · apologize

Canadian keeps the British -our ending (colour) but uses the American-style -ize ending (apologize). That mix is exactly what trips people up.

4 · Parts of speech & how to categorize a word

Every word gets filed into a word class (part of speech). The Week 2 "working scheme" below lists ten. (The course readings fold Noun and Pronoun into one family and set interjections aside — that's the "eight word classes" you'll also see mentioned.) Schemes differ between grammars anyway: categorizing 170,000 words is like sorting paint chips into "red, orange, yellow…" — the boundaries are a bit arbitrary, so some words sit on the line.

ClassExampleClassExample
NounstudentDeterminer (determinative)the
PronountheyPrepositionunder
VerblearnSubordinatorbecause
AdjectivepreciseCoordinatorand
AdverbcarefullyInterjectionwow

The four clues

Don't rely on meaning alone. Combine these, and always check the word in context:

ClueAskSignals
MeaningWhat does it express?Often useful, never enough on its own.
FormWhat endings can it take?plural -s · possessive -'s · -ly · -ous · -er/-est
PositionWhere can it sit?after a determiner; before a noun; etc.
ContextWhat's around it?the whole sentence settles it.
Your "grammar brain" already knows this

Read: The froominous meeps garkel voominously. You can label every made-up word without knowing what any of them mean:

froominous = adjective · meeps = noun · garkel = verb · voominously = adverb — proved by position and form, not meaning.

CheckIn The froominous meeps garkel voominously, what part of speech is garkel — and what tells you?
A verb.

Position: it comes right after the noun phrase the froominous meeps (the subject) and is followed by an -ly adverb, voominously. That's the slot only a verb can fill — no meaning required.

5 · Category vs. function

A giant screwdriver used to pry open paint cans is categorically a screwdriver but functionally a prybar. Words work the same way: a word's category (its word class) can differ from its function (the job it does in a sentence).

There must be a dog trainer who can teach Rex to roll over.
dog is categorically a noun, but here it is functioning as an adjective (an adjectival) — describing trainer.

Because a word can be one thing but do another, we give the functions their own names:

Category (word class)Function name
NounNominal
AdjectiveAdjectival
AdverbAdverbial
DeterminativeDeterminer
Verb · Pronoun · Coordinator · Subordinator— (no separate function name: only they can do their job)

Function names shine with phrases, not just words: in The sasquatch in the shed is asking for a snack, the phrase in the shed describes the sasquatch — it's an adjectival, even though it contains no adjective. So the more useful question in this course is "what is this word or phrase doing?" rather than "what class is it?"

Words drift

Categories aren't frozen. Adult (noun) now works as a verb (I have to adult today); access became a verb in the computer age. Speakers and influencers move words between classes over time.

CheckWhich of these has no separate function name (because only it can do its job): noun, adjective, or verb?
Verb.

Nouns → nominal and adjectives → adjectival, but only a verb can do a verb's job — so there's no "verbal" function slot other constituents can step into. (Pronouns, coordinators, and subordinators are the same.)

6 · POSTs — Parts-of-Speech Tests

A POST is a sentence pattern with one controlled blank. Your grammar brain only allows certain words into the slot, so the pattern tests how a word can function — without you having to define anything first. Fill She friendly and you'll only ever reach for a linking verb (is, seems, sounds), never jumps.

The four core POSTs from class, plus two more from the reading:

To find a…POST (fill the blank)Words that fit
NounMy is / are important.studies, health, curiosity, skiing, to learn
Linking verbShe friendly.is, seems, sounds
Intransitive verbShe expertly.sings, swims, performs
Transitive verbShe groceries frequently.buys, carries, orders
Determiner (mostly) pretty flowers grew on the hill.a, the, one, ten, many, some
Preposition (mostly)The squirrel the tree is annoyed.in, on, under, behind
How to read a POST result

A successful POST proves a possible function in context, not a permanent identity. One word can pass several: turned fits linking, intransitive, and transitive; owe only fits transitive. There are no reliable POSTs for pronouns, coordinators, or subordinators — and the noun POST is just the linking-verb pattern with the noun left blank.

CheckWhich word does not fit the noun POST "My is/are important": studies, quickly, skiing, health?
quickly.

quickly is an adverb and your grammar brain refuses it in that slot. studies and health are nouns; skiing fits too — but note it's a nominal (a verb form doing a noun's job), which is the next topic.

7 · Heads & dependents

Inside a phrase, one word is the boss:

  • The head is the central word that determines what kind of phrase it is.
  • A dependent is a word or group that adds to, completes, or limits the head. Remove the head and the phrase loses its anchor.
the · remarkably patient · student · near the window
Head: student. Dependents: the, remarkably patient, near the window. Note that remarkably depends on patient — not on student. Dependents can have dependents of their own, and a dependent can be a whole phrase.
The payoff — a comma rule

Never separate a head from its dependent with a comma. That single rule explains the two wrong commas in:

Chinook Lodge will be hosting the first, in what we hope will be a series, of workshops…

The commas cut the head first off from its dependent in what we hope will be a series of workshops. By the end of the course, you'll be able to name and fix errors like this.

CheckIn "the remarkably patient student near the window," what is the head, and what does remarkably depend on?
Head = student; remarkably depends on patient.

The phrase is about a student, so student is the head. remarkably modifies the adjective patient (how patient?), which in turn depends on student — a small chain of dependents.

8 · Nouns & nominals

The old "person, place, or thing" is too narrow. A noun can express far more kinds of "thing":

  • People & places — Janelle, Calgary, SAIT
  • Physical entities — hamster, rainbow, crash
  • Activities — bowling, golf, badminton
  • Concepts & qualities — science, rigour, softness
  • Mental processes — dreams, worries, love, boredom

Form & position clues: most nouns have singular/plural forms and can take a possessive -'s; they often follow a determiner and head a noun phrase. You know that merp likes the look of you makes merp a noun purely from its position — even with no idea what a merp is.

Nominals: non-nouns in a noun's slot

A nominal is a word or phrase that isn't a word-class noun but functions in a noun slot. They all pass the noun POST and can be replaced by it:

My laptop is important. (noun) · Skiing is important. (gerund) · To learn is important. (to-infinitive) · That you remembered my birthday is important. (whole clause)

A gerund is the -ing form doing a noun's job (skiing, singing).

CheckIn "Skiing is important," what is skiing — a verb, a noun, or a nominal? Why?
A nominal (specifically a gerund).

Skiing is built from a verb (ski + -ing), so it isn't a word-class noun — but it fills the subject/noun slot (it passes "My ___ is important" and can be replaced by it). A verb form doing a noun's job = a nominal.

9 · Verb forms & the jobs they do

Every verb has a set of forms (its paradigm) — think of it as a closet of uniforms the verb changes into for different jobs. The verb show has five:

FormNameMain jobs it can do
showplain (base)present tense · agreement (I/you/we/they) · after a modal (can show) · to-infinitive (to show)
shows-s / third-person singularpresent tense + agreement with he/she/it & singular nouns
showedpreterite (-ed)simple past tense
shownpast participle (-en)perfect verb strings (have shown) · adjectival (the paintings shown…)
showingpresent participle / gerund (-ing)progressive verb strings (is showing) · gerund/nominal · participle/modifier

Three things verb forms handle

  • Tense. English changes form for only two tenses — simple present and simple past. Everything else uses a verb string (more than one verb): will show, have shown, is showing, will have been waiting (four verbs!).
  • Agreement. Only the -s form marks it, and it agrees with he/she/it and singular nouns. (There is no such thing as a "plural verb," and the -s on a verb is not the plural -s on a noun.) The verb be is the exception — 8 forms, and 5 that show agreement: am, is, are, was, were.
  • "Non-verb" jobs. The -ing form can be a gerund (nominal) or a participle (adjectival/adverbial); the -en form can be adjectival; and plain + to makes a to-infinitive that acts as a nominal, adjectival, or adverbial.
CheckName the preterite of show, and say which form ends the perfect tenses (as in "have ___").
Preterite = showed · Perfect uses the past participle = shown.

showed is the simple past (I showed you). The perfect tenses are built with a verb string ending in the past participle / -en form: have shown, has shown. In "the paintings shown in the gallery," that same shown is instead doing a non-verb job — an adjectival.
